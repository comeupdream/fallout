# Six-Month Winter Grid-Down Plan — Two Adults

Working reference for a 180-day winter grid-down scenario (EMP-style total loss of power
and internet, no radiation). Two adults, 800 sq ft basement, firearms handled separately
and excluded here. Last updated 2026-08-29. Prices are 2026 street prices, ±20%.

**Core targets**

| Target | Number |
|---|---|
| Duration | 180 days through winter |
| Calories | 720,000 total (2,000/person/day — winter reality is 2,200–2,800 on cold/work days) |
| Water | ~500 gallons stored (drinking 180 at 0.5/p/d + cooking ~170 + hygiene ~110 + reserve 25) + replenishment stack |
| Ready-to-eat share | 20–30% of calories (no-cook or hot-water-only) |
| Heat | One insulated "warm room," not the whole basement |

---

## Why 180 days — the thesis behind the number

Winter isn't six months long; 180 days is an **acute-phase horizon**. Mortality
and unrest in a grid-down front-load, and the plan is shaped to that curve:

| Phase | What happens | Posture |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 0–2 | Shock. Most households hold <2 weeks of food; pharmacies days of stock. | Invisible. |
| Weeks 2–12 | **The peak.** Desperation violence as household food runs out; waterborne disease; the medically dependent (insulin, dialysis, O₂) mostly lost in 30–90 days. | Deepest lay-low. Light/smell/sound discipline. |
| Months 3–6 | Consolidation. Chaos burns calories it no longer has; winter suppresses movement; local order forms. | Careful radio listening → first contact with known neighbors. |
| Day ~180 | Spring. Gardening, foraging, travel, rebuilding become possible. | Emerge — toward community. |

**Confirmed:** surviving the 180 puts you past the statistical worst; the
survival curve is cliff-shaped and this plan climbs the cliff.

**Amended:** month 6 is not a threshold into a rebuilt world — it's the start of
a **years-long, regionally uneven recovery**. A regional event may see partial
restoration in weeks–months; a continental one is constrained by 12–24+ month
lead times on large power transformers. And the best real-world data (Puerto
Rico after Maria: 11 months to full restoration, ~3,000 excess deaths —
overwhelmingly medical/water, not violence; Sarajevo, post-Soviet collapse:
gardens, barter, and local networks carried survivors) says the recovery phase
rewards the *connected*, not the hidden. Plan an off-ramp, not a finish line:

- **Built-in stretch:** the food layer's ~1.03M calories = ~8.6 months at full
  ration, ~9.5 at 1,800 cal/day.
- **Taper rule:** no recovery signals (grid islands, organized relief, working
  barter) by month 4 → drop to 1,800/day and extend the horizon.
- **Off-ramp kit** (budget line added): heirloom seed vault, garden hand tools,
  canning starter, barter smalls — lighters, batteries, Aquatabs.
- **Emergence protocol:** months 4–6, scheduled GMRS listening, then contact
  with pre-identified neighbors — before anyone learns what the basement holds.

## 3. Water: retail-container storage + the four-layer purification stack

**Revised target: ~500 gal stored** (down from ~700). The math behind the cut:
FEMA's "1 gal/person/day" quietly *includes* hygiene — direct drinking for a
cool-basement, low-exertion life is genuinely ~0.5 gal — and this menu is soupy:
roughly a third of the ~170-gal cooking/rehydration budget ends up ingested with
the food. Three caveats keep it honest: the menu is salty (kits + canned run
4,000+ mg sodium/day — **drink to thirst, never ration below it**); cold
suppresses thirst while dry winter air raises losses; and one GI-sickness day
takes 1.5–2 gal/person (that's the reserve line). Hygiene thins but never to
zero — handwashing is disease prevention, and disease is the #1 grid-down killer.

| Line | Gal |
|---|---|
| Drinking, 0.5/person/day direct | 180 |
| Cooking/rehydration (kit layer ~110 of it; ~⅓ ingested with meals) | 170 |
| Hygiene, potable slice — bathing + handwash + final rinses (wash-grade water comes from snow + the cascade, §below) | 110 |
| Sickness/contingency reserve | 25 |
| **Stored target** | **~485 → 500** |

**Containers — the chosen buy: 180× 2.5-gal tap jugs @ $4 = $720 = 450 gal.**
Hits the stored target nearly on its own, and carries a ration discipline no
drum can match: **one jug per day for the household** — the remaining count IS
the days remaining, no measuring. Cost truth: taps at $1.60/gal are the priciest
way to store water (bottle cases ~$1.25/gal, drum capacity ~$1.45), so this buy
pays a ~$70 convenience premium over a case-heavy mix — worth it for the
spigots and the count-down discipline.

|---|---|---|---|

| 5-gal jerry cans — hauling layer; thin jugs don't survive refill trips | 10 | 50 | $150 |

Handling the 180: thin walls and 180 spigots are the failure surface. Single
layer only (loaded jugs don't stack) — ~4 shelf units, or floor pallets two
layers deep with a plywood separator between; drip trays or bins underneath,
cardboard removed; monthly leak inspection; drink through the stock on a 12–24
month cycle so no jug is ever older than two years. Buy 4–6 spares beyond the
180, and when a spigot weeps, consolidate that jug into pots the same day.
Jerry-can tap refills: rotate 12 mo or treat (~8 drops 6% bleach/gal).

**Purification layers** (any one can fail; each backs up the next):

1. **Gravity ceramic** — daily driver: AquaRain 404 or Doulton/British Berkefeld
   stainless gravity unit (~$300–330) + 4 spare candles. Thousands of gallons per
   candle. (Berkey itself has had regulatory/availability trouble since 2023–24 —
   if you already own one, just stock spare elements; if buying new, buy the above.)
2. **Hollow-fiber squeeze** — mobile: 2× Sawyer Squeeze (~$80/pair).
   **Winter-critical: a wet hollow-fiber filter is destroyed by a single freeze,
   with no visible damage.** Store above freezing; carry inside your coat when out.
   Wet ceramic candles can crack in a freeze too.
3. **Chemical** — bulk and barter-proof: 2× 1-lb bags of plain calcium hypochlorite
   pool shock, 68–73%, no algaecides/stabilizers (~$25 total, treats ~10,000+ gal).
   CDC method: 1 heaping tsp in 2 gal water = stock solution; then 1 part stock per
   100 parts water (≈1.3 oz/gal), wait 30 min, faint chlorine smell should remain.
   Cold water: wait 60 min. Store the shock sealed inside HDPE, ventilated, away
   from metals and every fuel — it's a strong oxidizer. Plus 100 Aquatabs for
   grab-and-go and a laminated dosing card.
4. **Boil** — rolling boil 1 min (3 min above ~6,500 ft). Works always, but boiling
   everything roughly doubles cooking fuel — that's why layers 1–3 exist.

**Winter sourcing:** snow melts ~10:1 (ten quarts snow → one quart water) — always
melt-then-treat, add a splash of water to the pot first so it doesn't scorch, and
never eat snow for hydration (core temperature loss). Mark the nearest surface water
on a paper map now. Basement storage won't freeze — another point for the basement.
The leaner 500-gal store leans harder on replenishment: have the gravity filter
assembled and producing by week 2–3, not when the shelves empty.

### Bathing & cleaning — the 0.5-gal protocol

**Yes: half a gallon a day bathes two people**, done exactly as described —
pre-soap the zones, cup-rinse. Four cups (1 qt) each is enough with technique.

**The daily zone bath (4 cups/person, ~5 minutes):**

1. Warm 2 qt on the stove to ~100°F (mix boiled + cold). Bathe in the warm
   room, standing in a big plastic tote — the catch water gets reused.
2. Cup 1 wets a washcloth: wipe face → neck → pits → groin → feet. Always
   clean → dirty, never backward.
3. Soap only the zones, soap diluted 1:4 — less soap is the secret to less rinse.
4. Cups 2–4 rinse in the same order, washcloth-assisted.
5. **Dry hard, then powder** (baking soda or medicated powder: pits, groin,
   feet) and clean underwear. Drying + powder prevent more disease than the
   wash — fungus breeds on damp skin, not dirty skin.

**Weekly rhythm (household):**

| Cadence | Wash | Water |
|---|---|---|
| Daily | Zone bath ×2 | 0.5 gal |
| Alternate days, optional swap | Wipe bath (bathing wipes) ×2 | 0 |
| Weekly | Full wash + hair day ×2 — cup method head-forward over the tote, or no-rinse shampoo caps | 2–3 gal |
| 2×/week | Socks + underwear laundry, bucket & plunger | 3–4 gal wash-grade |

**Water grades — why bathing doesn't drain the jugs.** Only drinking, cooking,
and final dish rinses draw the 180-jug stock. Bathing, laundry, and pre-washes
run on **wash-grade** water: snow melted passively (black trash bag of snow set
near — not on — the heater melts free while you do other things; wash water
doesn't need the fuel-heavy potable melt), filtered surface water, and the
cascade: bath-tote catch → laundry soak → bucket-toilet and floor cleaning.
Even "locked-up" winter is a water source for washing. Basement stock won't
freeze at 50°F.

**Stations.** The tap jugs literally are handwash stations: one jug + soap +
catch basin at the toilet, one at the cook zone. Sanitizer covers routine hand
events; real soap-and-water is mandatory after toilet duty and before cooking —
sanitizer is weak against norovirus, the disease that actually stalks grid-down
households. Dishes run minimal: kit meals eaten from the pouch, one bowl +
spoon per person wiped clean after use, pots scraped + wiped + boiled out
weekly, two-basin wash with a bleach-drop final rinse when needed.

**The medical stakes, plainly.** Six months of poor washing shows up as fungal
infection (groin, feet), boils, and — for the female partner — UTI risk:
front-to-back always, a daily plain-water peri rinse (soap outside only),
cotton underwear in rotation, and UTI treatment discussed with a physician for
the med kit, because an untreated UTI climbing to the kidneys is a genuine
grid-down emergency. Add to the buy list (~$60, inside the hygiene budget):
1,000+ bathing wipes, 8–10 no-rinse shampoo caps, 4 cans medicated body powder,
a large tote, and two dedicated washcloth/towel sets per person, dried by the
heater between uses.

## 4. Heat & fuel (with the safety corrections)

**Strategy:** heat the insulated warm room intermittently, sleep in cold-rated bags
(0°F to −20°F), and let the below-grade basement's natural 45–55°F do the rest.

**Hardware:** Mr. Heater Big Buddy MH18B (4/9/18k BTU, low-oxygen shutoff) primary +
Portable Buddy MH9BX backup (~$240 both), 12-ft hose + fuel filter through a window
board to the tank **outside**, two-burner propane stove, cast iron, and a wonder
oven / insulated retained-heat cooker (~$25 DIY) — bring rice/beans to a 5-minute
boil, then let insulation finish the cook. Cuts cooking fuel 50–70%.

**Burn math** (propane ≈ 21,600 BTU/lb; 20-lb tank ≈ 430,000 BTU):

| Setting | BTU/hr | lb/hr | Hours per 20-lb tank |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 4,000 | 0.19 | ~107 |
| Medium | 9,000 | 0.42 | ~48 |
| High | 18,000 | 0.83 | ~24 |

Budget 2.0–2.5 lb/day heating (insulated room, intermittent) + 0.5 lb/day cooking
≈ **3 lb/day worst case → 540 lb for 180 days**.

**The bank: 4× 100-lb cylinders + 6× 20-lb ≈ 520 lb (~$1,100–1,300 filled).**
100-lb cylinders are cheaper per pound and easier to chain up and lock. With
insulation discipline you'll run 1.5–2 lb/day and finish with a month-plus spare.

**Hard safety rules (the corrections to the earlier plan):**

- **No propane cylinder larger than 1 lb is ever stored indoors** (NFPA 58). The
  bank lives outside in a locked, ventilated enclosure, shaded, chained upright.
- **Gasoline indoors: zero gallons — not 20, not 5, and the basement least of
  all.** Vapor is 3–4× heavier than air and pools at the structure's lowest
  floor — the basement — where this plan runs an open-flame heater and stove
  and where you sleep; cans breathe vapor with every temperature swing, and
  ~1.4% concentration in a sealed room is ignitable. Codes that tolerate
  ~25 gal residential mean *detached* garages/sheds and prohibit basement
  storage outright. Winter argues for outdoor storage, not against it:
  gasoline doesn't freeze above roughly −40°F, and cold slows venting and
  aging — the locker is the good spot. The indoor-class fuels are kerosene
  (≤5 gal proper container; flash point above room temperature — why it's the
  historical indoor fuel) and 1-lb propane bottles. Refueling happens outside
  too. Gasoline's only jobs are the vehicle and barter.
  Sizing: **100 gal in 20× 5-gal cans is the right ceiling** — acute-phase
  driving is minimal (~5–10 gal over six months), a 400–500-mile relocation
  takes 25–35, post-acute mobility 30–50, barter the rest; 60 gal (12 cans) is
  the lean version. Buy ethanol-free, stabilize (PRI-G/Sta-Bil), rotate 2–3
  cans through the daily driver every 6 months, keep both vehicle tanks ≥¾ as
  standing habit, and store detached and locked — 100 gal exceeds many
  residential fire-code allowances, so site it away from the house or split
  locations. Most vehicles likely still run post-EMP (EMP Commission testing);
  keep the older, simpler one fueled.
- **Kerosene:** fine as a redundancy fuel (a Dyna-Glo/HeatMate convection heater +
  30 gal stored outside ≈ $450, optional) — max ~5 gal indoors in a proper blue can.
- **CO kills more people in outages than anything else on this page:** 3 battery CO
  alarms (one inside the warm room), 2 smoke alarms, 3 ABC extinguishers (warm
  room, cook zone, stairs), cracked-window ventilation whenever anything burns, and
  never sleep with the heater running.

**Fuel siting for this property — deck and balcony are OUT.** Anything attached
to the house (deck, balcony, attached garage) is off-limits for gasoline: a fuel
fire there IS a house fire, summer sun-load vents vapor continuously, and a
visible can wall is a theft and OPSEC problem. The three-tier architecture:

**Renter edition — no locker, no ownership, a slope pit for a couple cans is
the constraint. That still works; the plan re-sizes:**

1. **Vehicle tanks — 25–30 gal — are now the PRIMARY store.** Purpose-built
   steel, mobile, zero footprint, zero landlord issues. Both vehicles kept
   ≥¾ by weekly top-offs; that habit alone holds most of the acute-phase and
   relocation fuel.
2. **The slope pit — 2–4 cans, 10–20 gal.** Minimal build done right: cut into
   the slope 20+ ft from the house and NOT uphill of it (leaks flow downhill —
   they must flow away from structures, wells, streams); 4–6" gravel bed that
   drains outward; a hard liner (poly job box, or dry-stacked cinder block
   with a paver lid); vent gaps or a screened pipe high and low — never
   airtight; soil and leaf litter back over, sited off any sightline. E0 only,
   double-dose PRI-G, dated cans, poured through the car and refilled every
   6–12 months (small stock = easy rotation). Renter reality: keep it fully
   reversible — cut and save the sod, restore on move-out — and a compost bin
   or firewood rack beside it is honest-looking cover.
3. **Off-site cans — optional 10–20 gal** at a trusted friend's or family
   property, which also seeds the plan's off-site cache. Restores capacity
   with zero on-site footprint.

Cold storage in the pit is good for the gas (slows venting and aging), and
below-grade it never freezes solid — gasoline stays liquid to roughly −40°F
anyway.

**Mileage budget: 1,000–1,500 miles is right, and most of it is held, not
driven.** Acute phase ≈ 100–300 mi total (a last-chance run or two, rare
medical, months-3–6 scouting); a 300–500-mi relocation reserve stays untouched;
recovery months 6–12 add 600–1,200 as life resumes. The renter-sized 35–70 gal
supply is 500–1,400 mi at truck mileage — the acute phase and the full
relocation reserve are covered; deep recovery-era driving waits for fuel to
reappear commercially, and the barter tier moves to batteries, lighters, and
Aquatabs, which trade better per pound anyway. Rules of the road: every acute-phase trip is exposure
(engine noise signature, chokepoints, visible fuel), so batch errands and go
driver-plus-observer; a **monthly 10–15-min maintenance run** (open air, never
a garage) is the battery tender now — budget 3–5 gal for it; **never idle for
cabin heat** (CO + the worst fuel trade in the plan); and a tuned **bicycle**
owns the 1–5 mile band — zero fuel, low signature, the vehicle of every
post-collapse economy on record.

Total effective: **35–70 gal with zero indoor storage — enough for what
gasoline actually does in this plan.** The dig-and-landscape instinct
generalizes well beyond fuel: an earth-sheltered cache is the right home for
the plan's off-site 5–10% — sealed food buckets, water cached below the frost
line, tools, barter smalls. Two exceptions: propane never gets buried (tanks
rust; it lives above ground in shade), and nothing that needs monthly rotation
goes underground.

**Propane knock-on:** the no-locker constraint hits the heat bank too. Two
renter-fit answers to pick between when the heat block gets itemized:
(a) an **all-20-lb fleet** — ten to twelve BBQ tanks upright along a fence
line or behind shrubs reads as completely ordinary, takes ~3×4 ft, and cable-
locks to a post; or (b) a **kerosene-primary pivot** — denser storage in small
blue cans, ≤5 gal legally indoors, heater ~$160 — which shrinks outdoor fuel
footprint to almost nothing. What the deck and balcony ARE good for:
firewood, tarps, empty containers, and three-season rain barrels — nothing
flammable, and in winter nothing that bursts when frozen.

## 5. Refined budget — three builds, two adults

Lean = viable with discipline; Recommended = the plan as written; Premium = margin
everywhere. Your earlier $8.2–8.4k estimate is essentially the Lean build — the gap

## 6. Skills & the dry run

Gear is the smaller half. Before winter: Stop the Bleed + CPR courses, water dosing
from memory, radio discipline drills with the GMRS pair (listen much, transmit
little, nothing about supplies on air; $35 GMRS license, no test), bread/beans/rice
cookery on the actual stove, and cold-injury recognition. Print *Where There Is No
Doctor* and the dosing card.

**The #1 recommendation in this whole document:** run a 72-hour full dry run in
November — main breaker off, phones in the Faraday can, live only from the basement
plan. It will find every hole in this document for $0.
